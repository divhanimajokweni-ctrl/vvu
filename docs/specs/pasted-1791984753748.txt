Anatomy × VVU / ProofBridge

Review, integration strategy, and project opportunities

My assessment: Anatomy could become a useful visualisation and explanation layer across your upcoming projects, particularly your Proof Spatial Intelligence dashboard, VVU·IVE, ProofBridge, and agricultural edge systems.

The important distinction is that Anatomy is not a spatial intelligence engine, a digital twin platform, or an evidence verification system. It is a tool for constructing detailed, interactive visual representations of technical systems.

Its value to you is that it can help turn otherwise abstract system behaviour into something an operator can see, manipulate, and understand.

I inspected the uploaded anatomy-main.zip, including its README, primary skill instructions, examples, rendering toolkit, verification scripts, and licence.

I would approach integration as a reusable visualisation capability rather than merging the entire repository into every project.


---

1. What you actually have in the ZIP

The archive contains a complete software repository with 169 filesystem entries, including eight example projects, reusable rendering modules, an agent skill, documentation, and automated tests.

The central idea behind Anatomy is simple:

Instead of explaining a technical concept with abstract boxes and arrows, represent it as a physical object whose parts and movements explain how the system works.

For example, a rate limiter can become a mechanical device that regulates incoming requests. A verification process can become an instrument with moving components and measurable states.

The object is not merely decorative. Its movement and displayed measurements are intended to correspond to a shared underlying model.

That distinction makes Anatomy particularly interesting for your work.

This is more valuable than a collection of illustrations. It gives you several implementation patterns to study and reuse.

Three capabilities stand out

1. Detailed SVG construction

The renderer builds objects from many individual components, with depth ordering, shading, and perspective.

SVG is particularly suitable for interactive dashboards because its elements can be individually positioned, labelled, animated, and connected to application state.

2. Motion driven by a model

The instructions emphasise that the displayed measurements and movements should derive from the same underlying model.

That principle aligns with your preference for dashboards in which an operator can inspect system behaviour rather than simply being shown an unexplained status indicator.

3. Geometric auditing

The toolkit contains mechanisms for examining overlaps, component relationships, drawing order, and other geometric properties.

These are useful checks on the visual construction itself. They do not establish that the depicted physical system is correctly modelled or that its data is trustworthy.

That limitation becomes important when we connect Anatomy to ProofBridge.

2. The central integration principle

I would give Anatomy a specific role in your architecture:

Anatomy renders the system. VVU·IVE determines the system's state. ProofBridge supplies the evidence that supports that state.

This separation is essential.

Consider a sensor reporting a possible water leak on a farm.

The underlying system might contain:

A sensor measuring water flow.

A baseline model estimating expected consumption.

A detector identifying an unusual deviation.

An evidence pipeline collecting observations.

A verification process checking whether the observations support the claim.

An operator dashboard displaying the result.


Anatomy could represent the pipe, sensor, valve, and measurement instrument as a detailed interactive figure.

But Anatomy should not decide whether a leak has been verified.

That decision belongs to your application and verification logic.

The visualisation should display the result produced by those systems, including uncertainty and missing evidence.

The adapter between your application and Anatomy is the important new component.

It translates your existing system state into the properties needed to render a figure.

For example:

const visualState = {
  system: "farm-water-monitor",
  status: "INCONCLUSIVE",

  sensor: {
    connected: true,
    reading: 128.4,
    unit: "L/min"
  },

  baseline: {
    available: true,
    expected: 96.0,
    unit: "L/min"
  },

  evidence: {
    sourceCount: 2,
    independentSources: 1,
    provenanceAvailable: true
  },

  verification: {
    passed: false,
    missingInputs: ["independent corroboration"]
  }
};

These values are illustrative, not measurements from an actual farm.

An adapter could use this state to move a gauge needle, highlight a pipe, show the observed deviation, and display the verification status.

The figures should update when the underlying state changes.

The important constraint is that the adapter must not invent a verification result merely because the animation looks convincing.

3. Application one: Proof Spatial Intelligence dashboard

This is where I would begin.

Your spatial intelligence dashboard needs to communicate relationships between physical locations, assets, measurements, and operational events.

Anatomy offers a way to represent individual technical mechanisms in greater detail than a conventional dashboard card.

I would explore three complementary uses.

A. Spatial infrastructure figures

Imagine an operator selecting a location on a spatial map.

Instead of seeing only a collection of markers and measurements, the operator can open a detailed figure of the relevant infrastructure.

For example, a water-monitoring installation might reveal:

A storage tank.

An inlet and outlet.

Connected pipes.

Flow sensors.

A pump and valve.

A local edge device.

A communications link to the central platform.


Each component could display its current state.

Selecting a sensor could open its evidence history. Selecting a pipe could reveal the measurements associated with that segment.

The figure becomes a navigable representation of the system's physical structure.

What Anatomy contributes

Its isometric geometry, component construction, and interactive motion could provide the detailed asset view.

What your spatial application contributes

Your existing spatial data, coordinate systems, asset identifiers, map interactions, and relationships between infrastructure components.

I would not use Anatomy to replace the spatial engine. I would use it to provide a detailed equipment view associated with the selected spatial object.

A map and an isometric equipment figure answer different questions:

The map answers: Where is the asset?

The figure answers: How is the asset constructed, and what is happening inside it?


That is a useful division of responsibility.

B. A visual provenance explorer

This is the more interesting opportunity for ProofBridge.

Imagine an operator investigating a claim about a water leak.

The dashboard could display the relevant equipment and its evidence chain.

An observation arrives from a sensor. A second observation comes from another source. A baseline model compares the measurements. A verification stage determines whether the available evidence satisfies the required conditions.

An interactive instrument could represent those stages physically.

For example:

A measurement dial represents the observed deviation.

A source selector identifies which sensor produced the observation.

Separate evidence channels represent the available sources.

A mechanical interlock represents a verification condition.

A final indicator shows whether the claim is proven, inconclusive, or blocked.


The interlock must be driven by the actual verification result, not by the illustration's own logic.

If independent corroboration is missing, the mechanism should remain visibly blocked.

This would give your Evidence Independence Specification a concrete operator-facing representation without confusing the illustration with the specification itself.

C. Spatial incident reconstruction

A third possibility is to use Anatomy to explain how a spatial incident developed.

Suppose a series of sensors detects a temperature excursion across a cold-chain installation.

A spatial interface could show where the affected assets are. An Anatomy figure could show the refrigeration mechanism and its sensors.

A timeline could then replay the incident:

1. The temperature begins rising.


2. The sensor reports a deviation.


3. The edge node records the observation.


4. A second source supplies corroborating or contradictory evidence.


5. The verification process evaluates the claim.


6. The operator receives the final result.



The replay would be useful for incident investigation, operator training, and technical reviews.

However, the timeline must be driven by recorded event timestamps and actual system events. Animation speed must not be confused with real elapsed time.

4. Application two: VVU·IVE and the Evidence Runtime

I see a particularly good fit between Anatomy and your existing Evidence Runtime concept.

Your runtime has explicit verification stages, blocked states, evidence requirements, and release decisions.

Those are abstract concepts that are difficult to communicate through a conventional collection of status badges.

Anatomy could give them a physical interface.

Concept: The Evidence Instrument

Imagine a precision instrument mounted inside the Evidence Runtime panel.

Its base represents the verification boundary. Several independently movable mechanisms represent the evidence sources and verification conditions.

As the runtime evaluates a claim, the instrument changes state.

Runtime condition	Possible visual representation

Evidence received	An input carriage moves into position
Provenance missing	A source-identification mechanism remains disengaged
Required input missing	An interlock prevents the verification mechanism from engaging
Independence requirement satisfied	Separate evidence channels reach their required positions
Verification completed	The final indicator moves to the corresponding result
Release blocked	A physical stop prevents the release lever from moving


These are design proposals, not features already implemented by Anatomy.

The figure could offer a compact overview while allowing the operator to inspect individual components.

Why this matters

Your underlying philosophy is that a system should expose the evidence supporting its outputs instead of asking people to trust a status label.

A conventional dashboard might display:

VERIFICATION: INCONCLUSIVE

An interactive instrument could additionally show why the verification is inconclusive.

For example, the missing independent source could be represented by a visibly incomplete input channel.

The operator would be able to inspect the condition responsible for the result.

That is a much stronger explanation than an animated green or red indicator alone.

One critical rule

The renderer must never be allowed to promote an epistemic state.

If VVU·IVE says BLOCKED_MISSING_INPUT, the visual layer cannot turn the result into PROVEN because a user pressed a button or because an animation reached its final frame.

The visual state is a representation of the authoritative state, not an alternative decision-making engine.

This is where your Evidence Independence Specification and Anatomy must remain separate.

5. Application three: ProofBridge's verification and provenance interfaces

ProofBridge has another potential use for Anatomy: explaining how evidence moves through a verification pipeline.

Your five-pass workflow is:

1. Collect.


2. Boundary-check.


3. Baseline.


4. Independence.


5. Export.



A conventional pipeline diagram could represent these stages as five boxes.

An Anatomy-based interface could instead represent them as five interconnected mechanisms within a single physical apparatus.

For example, the apparatus might resemble a precision inspection machine.

Evidence enters through an intake mechanism. The boundary-check stage directs it through an inspection gate. The baseline stage compares it with a reference. The independence stage checks the required source relationships. The export mechanism releases the resulting verification artefact only when the required conditions are met.

The physical metaphor should follow the actual verification semantics.

A failed boundary check, for example, should not be depicted as successful merely because the evidence proceeds to the next animation.

What the operator could inspect

Selecting a mechanism could reveal:

The actual input received.

The relevant processing stage.

The verification conditions applied.

The recorded result.

The associated provenance references.

Any missing inputs or unresolved assumptions.


Those details should come from the underlying verification records.

The figure itself should not become a second source of truth.

A useful design distinction

I would separate the interface into three layers.

Layer 1: Mechanism

The Anatomy figure shows how the pipeline operates.

Layer 2: Evidence

A conventional panel displays the actual evidence, provenance references, timestamps, and verification results.

Layer 3: Decision

The application displays the authoritative acceptance or release decision.

This allows the visualisation to improve understanding without obscuring the underlying artefacts.

It also makes the design more defensible in a technical review: an evaluator can inspect the actual evidence rather than having to accept the visualisation's interpretation.

6. Application four: Your agricultural edge systems

Your agricultural projects are another strong candidate because they connect physical equipment, local computation, and operational decisions.

Anatomy could provide a visual interface for the physical systems that your edge nodes monitor.

Consider your proposed ESP32-S3 edge node and the broader agricultural monitoring architecture.

A detailed figure could show:

The sensor assembly.

The controller.

Power input and battery.

Solar charging components.

Storage.

Communications hardware.

The monitored equipment or environment.


The interface could then expose live measurements and system health.

For example, selecting the power subsystem could display battery voltage, charging status, and the last reported measurement.

Selecting a soil sensor could display its latest reading, calibration status, and measurement history.

The underlying hardware would remain unchanged. Anatomy would supply a visual representation of the equipment and its operating state.

An important distinction: simulation versus telemetry

Suppose the figure shows a pump running.

There are at least three possible meanings:

1. The pump is physically running, as confirmed by a sensor.


2. The application has issued a command requesting that the pump run.


3. A demonstration simulation is showing what the pump would do.



These states must not be visually interchangeable.

I would explicitly distinguish them in the interface.

For example, the application could use a separate visual treatment for simulated motion, requested operation, and telemetry-confirmed operation.

That is especially important in agricultural systems, where an attractive interface must not create the false impression that a physical actuator has responded successfully.

Where WebGL might help

The repository includes examples of fluid motion, lighting, water, and exhaust effects.

Those techniques could help illustrate:

Water flowing through irrigation pipes.

Thermal movement through a cooling system.

Airflow through ventilation equipment.

A refrigeration system's operating cycle.


However, these effects would initially be explanatory visualisations.

They would not constitute validated fluid dynamics, thermal simulation, or physical predictions unless connected to an appropriate model and validated against the real system.

For an early agricultural product, I would prioritise accurate sensor state, equipment relationships, and clear fault explanations over elaborate effects.

8. How I would integrate the code

The first technical decision is whether to adopt Anatomy as an AI development skill, a rendering library, or both.

These are related but distinct uses.

Option A: Install the skill for development

The repository is designed to be installed as an agent skill for compatible development environments, including Claude Code, OpenCode, and Codex.

Its SKILL.md teaches the agent how to design figures, select geometry, construct objects, and validate its work.

This is useful if you want an AI coding agent to generate new visualisations for different projects.

The installation does not automatically add the resulting figures to your dashboard. It gives the agent instructions and the supporting examples and code.

The repository documents installation instructions and prerequisites in its README.

You can consult the author's official repository here: [Anatomy repository](https://github.com/wheresryan22/anatomy?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

Option B: Integrate the rendering toolkit

This is the more important step for your product architecture.

You would select the necessary rendering modules and build a small adapter around them.

I would initially use this structure:

The objective is to isolate Anatomy from your business logic.

The dashboard would continue to own navigation, authentication, layout, and authoritative application state.

The adapter would translate the relevant state into a format the figures understand.

The figures would render that state and expose defined interactions.

That means you could replace a figure without rewriting the verification engine.

It also means you could change the verification implementation without rebuilding the entire visualisation system.

Option C: Use the generated figures as standalone HTML

Anatomy supports standalone HTML output, which is useful for early experiments.

You could create a proof of concept before introducing React or Next.js integration.

For example, a standalone figure could demonstrate how the Evidence Runtime instrument should look and behave.

Once the concept works, it could be integrated into the dashboard.

This is probably the least complicated way to establish whether Anatomy provides enough value to justify deeper integration.

9. Compatibility with your existing dashboard

Given your existing dashboard architecture, I would not replace the application shell or restructure the entire project around Anatomy.

I would treat it as an embedded visualisation component.

Your existing dashboard remains responsible for:

Navigation and routing.

Authentication and session handling.

The visual shell and layout.

Evidence retrieval.

Verification state.

Tenant and access controls.

Release decisions.


Anatomy would be responsible for:

Detailed technical figures.

Isometric geometry.

Component-level motion.

Interactive visual controls.

Optional WebGL effects.

Visual construction checks.


The interface between them should be explicit.

For example, the dashboard could provide a figure with a state snapshot and a callback for user interaction. The figure could request that the application open an evidence record, select a sensor, or display a particular component.

It should not directly mutate the authoritative verification state.

This boundary would allow Anatomy to coexist with your existing dashboard rather than becoming a competing application framework.

A note about performance

The repository includes sophisticated examples with many geometric components and some per-frame three-dimensional calculations.

I would not assume that every figure will perform equally well on every device.

Your mobile users and lower-cost agricultural hardware make this particularly relevant.

The initial integration should measure:

Rendering time.

Animation frame rate.

Memory use.

Performance on mobile browsers.

Behaviour when the dashboard contains several figures.

Behaviour when the underlying state updates frequently.


Static geometry should be reused where possible. Only genuinely changing geometry should be rebuilt each frame.

The simpler SVG examples are a better starting point than the three-dimensional mode.

11. A practical integration roadmap

I would not attempt to integrate all eight examples into all your projects at once.

Instead, I would establish one reusable integration and then expand it.

Phase 1 — Build one working figure

Target: the VVU·IVE Evidence Runtime.

Create a single instrument that represents an actual verification process.

Start with SVG and a small, explicitly defined state model.

Use representative states such as:

READY

BLOCKED_MISSING_INPUT

INCONCLUSIVE

PROVEN


These are illustrative application states; the final vocabulary must match the authoritative runtime.

The first version should demonstrate that the figure can receive a state update and render the corresponding result without independently deciding that result.

Deliverable: one working, interactive figure embedded in a test page.

Phase 2 — Create the reusable adapter

Separate the figure from its demonstration data.

Define a stable interface between the application and the renderer.

The interface should support:

Initial state.

Subsequent state updates.

Evidence references.

Component selection.

Interaction events.

Rendering errors.

Simulation versus live-data mode.


At this point, the figure should no longer depend on hard-coded demonstration values.

Deliverable: a reusable Anatomy integration component.

Phase 3 — Connect it to ProofBridge

Connect the adapter to actual verification outputs.

For every displayed result, preserve the reference to the underlying evidence or verification record.

Test the figure against positive, negative, incomplete, and contradictory evidence scenarios.

The key acceptance test is that the visual representation faithfully reflects the authoritative state, including blocked and inconclusive outcomes.

Deliverable: an evidence-aware visualisation that does not create its own verification decisions.

Phase 4 — Extend to spatial intelligence

Introduce spatial asset selection.

When an operator selects an asset on the map, open the associated Anatomy figure.

Map sensor identifiers, asset relationships, and measurements into the visual model.

Keep spatial positioning and geographic operations in the existing spatial system.

Deliverable: a spatial dashboard that can move between geographic context and detailed equipment inspection.

Phase 5 — Reuse the integration in agriculture

Once the interface is stable, introduce agricultural figures.

The first candidate should be a monitoring node or water system, because either can connect physical equipment to measurable operational state.

Later, you can add more specialised figures and WebGL effects where they provide genuine value.

Deliverable: a reusable visualisation capability shared across multiple VVU projects.

12. The bigger opportunity: a visualisation system for VVU

The most interesting long-term possibility is that Anatomy becomes the starting point for a VVU visualisation library.

I would not simply maintain a folder of independent illustrations.

I would develop a set of reusable visualisation patterns.

For example:

Visualisation pattern	Purpose	Potential applications

Evidence Instrument	Explains verification conditions	VVU·IVE, ProofBridge
Spatial Asset	Shows equipment and its components	Proof Spatial Intelligence
Provenance Mechanism	Explains relationships between observations and claims	Evidence Runtime, incident investigations
Sensor Assembly	Shows sensors, power, and communications	Agricultural edge systems
Circuit Breaker	Explains a blocked operation or failed condition	Release controls, system safety
Temporal Replay	Shows recorded events in sequence	Incident reconstruction
Resource Mechanism	Explains capacity, allocation, and utilisation	Infrastructure and operational dashboards


Each pattern would use the same basic integration contract.

The data model would describe the system. The application would determine what the data means. The renderer would turn that state into a comprehensible visual representation.

That separation is what would make the work reusable.

It would also prevent you from having to invent a new architecture whenever a project needs a detailed interactive figure.

One further opportunity: visualisation with evidence provenance

You could go a step further and associate every important visual element with a source record.

For example, selecting a temperature gauge could open the exact observation that produced its displayed reading.

Selecting a verification interlock could open the verification record that determines whether it is engaged.

Selecting an asset could open its registered identity and provenance history.

This would connect the operator's visual experience directly to your evidence architecture.

It would not make the figure itself a cryptographic proof. The underlying records, verification logic, and integrity mechanisms would still have to establish authenticity and correctness.

But it would make those records easier to inspect.

13. My recommendation

I would adopt Anatomy in two roles.

First, use it as a development skill. It can guide an AI coding agent in producing consistent, detailed technical figures rather than repeatedly inventing new visual styles and drawing approaches.

Second, selectively integrate its rendering toolkit. Build a small adapter so that the figures can display real application state without taking ownership of your business logic.

I would begin with the Evidence Runtime, not the agricultural node or the spatial map.

The Evidence Runtime is the best first experiment because its states and constraints are comparatively explicit. You can test whether the figure faithfully represents them before introducing geographic complexity, physical telemetry, or more elaborate simulation.

Once that works, the same integration can support your other projects.

The architectural principle I would preserve throughout is:

The system establishes what is true, the evidence explains why the system believes it, and the visualisation makes both inspectable.

Anatomy can help with that third responsibility and, when carefully connected to your existing systems, make the first two easier for an operator to understand.

It should not be asked to replace them.
